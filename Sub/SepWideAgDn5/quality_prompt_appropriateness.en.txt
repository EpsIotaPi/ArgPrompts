Eleven quality dimensions are defined as follows.
Local Acceptability: Are the premises of the argument worthy of being believed on rational grounds?
Local Relevance: Assuming the premises are true, can they all serve as reasons or evidence relevant to the conclusion?
Local Sufficiency: Assuming the premises are true, do they together provide enough support to make it rational to accept the conclusion?
Credibility: Does the way that the argument is expressed make the author seem trustworthy and sufficiently knowledgeable about the issue?
Emotional Appeal: Does the argument evoke the reader's emotions in a way that makes the reader more willing to accept the author's argument?
Clarity: Is the language of the argument clear, so that the reader can understand exactly what the author claims and how the author argues for it?
Appropriateness: Are the wording and tone of the argument appropriate and proportionate to the seriousness of the issue and the setting of the discussion?
Arrangement: Does the argument organize the issue, premises, conclusion, and other components in a sensible order, with suitable transitions between them?
Global Acceptability: Would someone who holds the opposing position also accept the author discussing the issue with these premises and in this way?
Global Relevance: Is the argument relevant to the issue under discussion, and does it help move the issue toward a resolution?
Global Sufficiency: Does the argument raise opposing views and respond to them adequately (including implicit responses)?
